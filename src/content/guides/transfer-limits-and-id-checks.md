## Why do transfer services check identity at all?

Money transfer companies are regulated financial businesses. In the United States they are "money services businesses" under the Bank Secrecy Act; in the EU and UK they are payment or e-money institutions under anti-money-laundering law. All of them must know who their customers are, keep records, screen against sanctions lists and report suspicious activity. Identity checks are not a sign that something is wrong with your payment. They are the price of a licence to move money.

What differs between providers is how they implement the rules. Some verify you with a photo ID and a selfie before your first transfer; others let you send a small amount first and verify later. Limits usually rise as you verify more.

## What triggers extra questions?

Several thresholds are written into law. In the US:

- **3,000 dollars.** For funds transfers of 3,000 dollars or more, 31 CFR 1010.410 requires the institution sending the transfer to obtain and keep specific information about the sender and recipient, and to verify the identity of a sender who is not an established customer.
- **More than 10,000 dollars in cash.** Under 31 CFR 1010.311, financial institutions, including money services businesses, must file a currency transaction report for cash transactions over 10,000 dollars in a day.

In the EU, Regulation (EU) 2023/1113 requires the payer's and payee's names and account details to travel with every transfer of funds, and extra verification applies above set amounts. The UK Money Laundering Regulations 2017 require customer due diligence and enhanced checks where risk is higher.

On top of these legal triggers, providers run their own risk models. Common reasons for a pause are a first transfer, a new recipient, a payment much larger than your usual pattern, a high-risk destination, or a name that resembles one on a sanctions list.

## What is a source-of-funds check?

For larger amounts a provider may ask where the money came from. This is a normal part of enhanced due diligence. Examples of evidence that usually works:

| Situation | Typical evidence |
|---|---|
| Savings from salary | Recent payslips and a bank statement showing the balance building up |
| Property or car sale | The sale contract and the bank statement showing the proceeds |
| Inheritance or gift | A will, probate letter or gift letter with the giver's bank statement |
| Business or freelance income | Invoices, a tax return or business bank statements |

Send clear, complete documents the first time. Partial or cropped statements are the most common reason a review takes longer than a day. For timing in general, see [how long transfers take](/guides/how-long-international-transfers-take/).

## Why are my limits lower than someone else's?

Provider limits typically depend on:

1. **Verification level.** Unverified or partly verified accounts get low caps; fully verified accounts get higher ones.
2. **Payment method.** Card funding is often capped lower than bank transfer funding, because card payments can be disputed and reversed.
3. **Destination and payout type.** Cash pickup and mobile wallet payouts often have lower per-transfer limits than bank deposits, sometimes set by the receiving country or partner.
4. **Time window.** Many providers apply daily, monthly and rolling limits at the same time.

If you plan a large payment, such as a property deposit or tuition, check the provider's limit page and verify your account a week ahead. A bank wire may suit a one-off very large payment if the provider caps below what you need; compare the true cost in [bank international transfer fees](/guides/bank-international-wire-fees/).

## Why splitting a payment is a bad idea

Breaking a large cash payment into smaller ones to stay under a reporting threshold is called structuring. Under 31 U.S.C. 5324 it is a federal offence in its own right, even if the money is entirely legal. Providers watch for it and may close accounts that show the pattern. If an amount is large, send it in one transfer and provide the documents.

Splitting also tends to cost more: flat fees are paid several times, and smaller amounts often get a wider exchange-rate markup. In the [calculator](/) you can see how the percentage cost changes between 200, 1,000 and 5,000.

## Recipient checks matter too

Many delays come from the receiving side. The recipient's name must match the name on their bank or wallet account, including middle names in some countries. In the euro area, the Instant Payments Regulation requires banks to offer a free verification-of-payee check before you send, which flags a mismatch between the name and the IBAN; the UK has a similar Confirmation of Payee service. Treat a "no match" result as a reason to confirm details with the recipient, not to override.

Destination countries can add their own rules, such as purpose codes for incoming payments or limits on how much a mobile wallet can receive. The [India guide](/guides/send-money-to-india/) covers the specifics for one of the largest corridors.

## A short checklist before a large transfer

- Verify your account fully before you need it.
- Check the provider's per-transfer and monthly limits for your payment method.
- Prepare source-of-funds evidence for amounts well above your usual transfers.
- Confirm the recipient's exact account name and number in writing.
- Send in one transfer rather than splitting it.
